Cold Chain Performance: PP Corrugated vs. Cardboard Produce Boxes

Cardboardproduceboxes Editorial • September 2026

Cold Chain Packaging: Where Box Material Actually Matters

The fresh produce cold chain is unforgiving. From pre-cooling rooms to refrigerated trailers to grocery distribution centers, produce packaging must perform across a wide range of temperatures and humidity levels — often for 5 to 14 days between harvest and retail. The material your box is made from directly affects produce shelf life, box integrity, and the total number of usable trips a reusable container can deliver.

This guide compares PP corrugated produce boxes against wax-coated cardboard in cold chain conditions, using the performance dimensions procurement teams and plant managers care about most.

Temperature Range Performance

Polypropylene corrugated material maintains dimensional stability across a range of approximately -20°C to +80°C (-4°F to +176°F). In practical cold chain terms, this means PP corrugated boxes perform without distortion or weakening in forced-air pre-cooling rooms, refrigerated trailer transport, cold-room storage at distribution centers, and tropical harvest environments before pre-cooling.

Cardboard — even wax-coated — absorbs moisture over time in high-humidity environments, leading to progressive compression strength loss. Agricultural engineering research has shown cardboard can lose 50–75% of its stacking strength when wet, directly contributing to box crush and produce damage in transit.

Moisture Resistance and Structural Integrity

Wax-coated cardboard was designed to address the moisture problem, but it's an imperfect solution. Wax coating adds cost, makes the box non-recyclable, and still allows moisture ingress through score lines, flaps, and cut edges over time. In ice-in-box applications common for seafood and some field crops, wax boxes degrade significantly by the end of transit.

PP corrugated is inherently moisture-resistant. The twin-wall polypropylene structure does not absorb water, swell, or lose compressive strength when exposed to condensation, ice melt, or direct water contact during washing. For operations using hydrocooling or ice packing, this is a fundamental structural advantage that directly reduces produce loss claims.

Buyers sourcing PP corrugated produce boxes should request stacking load specifications at operating temperature ranges. A reputable manufacturer will provide compression test data across a temperature spectrum, not just at room temperature.

Stacking Strength: What the Numbers Mean

Cold chain stacking is typically 8–12 boxes high on a standard pallet, with pallet weights ranging from 800 to 1,200 lbs for produce. The bottom box must sustain the full stack load continuously for up to two weeks in variable conditions.

PP corrugated boxes are specified by wall caliper — typically 4mm, 6mm, or 8mm flute — and resin density. A standard 6mm twin-wall PP corrugated box designed for produce will typically sustain 500–800 kg static compression at room temperature, retaining a high percentage of that strength at refrigerated temperatures because PP does not absorb moisture.

By contrast, cardboard boxes are rated by Edge Crush Test (ECT) or Bursting Strength, but these ratings assume dry conditions. In-cold-chain performance may be significantly lower. For procurement teams writing packaging specifications, requiring a wet-condition compression test for any cardboard supplier is a reasonable due-diligence standard.

Ventilation Design and Airflow in Cold Chain

Ventilation is critical in produce cold chain. Pre-cooling efficiency depends on airflow through the box stack, and vent hole design directly affects cooling rate and uniformity. PP corrugated boxes can be manufactured with precisely positioned vent holes that maintain structural integrity around cutouts — something harder to achieve with wet cardboard where vent edges can tear or collapse under load.

Condensation is also an operational concern when boxes move from cold rooms to ambient temperature during loading and unloading. PP corrugated handles condensation without surface degradation, while cardboard may soften at print areas or closure points, compromising stack stability during the transition period.

Performance Summary

  • Moisture resistance: PP corrugated is inherently waterproof; cardboard degrades over time even when waxed
  • Stacking strength in wet or cold conditions: PP corrugated maintains rated strength; cardboard loses 50–75% when wet
  • Temperature range: PP stable from -20°C to +80°C; cardboard performance narrows significantly in cold, humid conditions
  • Reusability: PP corrugated typically 50–100+ trips; cardboard is single-use
  • Total cost: Higher upfront per unit for PP, but significantly lower cost-per-trip over the operational life of the container
